# Discovering Market Regimes Through Structure

Markets don't behave the same way every day. Sometimes everything moves together—up or down in unison. Other times, sectors rotate: tech falls while energy rises. These patterns repeat. Not exactly, but structurally.

Your task in this project is to **discover** whether market movements live in a few dominant patterns, and if so, to **see** those patterns as geometric structure. You will not predict anything. You will not optimize anything. You will simply look at real market behavior and ask: *what structure is hiding in these movements?*

The only tools you need are vectors, matrices, and the geometric intuition you've built.

---

## Section 0: What Is a Market Regime?

Before we touch any math, let's build intuition about what we're looking for.

Imagine watching the market over 500 trading days. Each day, you record how each of 10 assets moved—some up, some down, by various amounts. That's 500 observations of 10-dimensional behavior.

Now ask: **do those 500 days look random, or do they cluster into a few recurring patterns?**

A "regime" isn't about whether prices go up or down. It's about the **structure** of how assets move **together**. Two days belong to the same regime if their movements share the same underlying pattern—even if the magnitudes differ.

## Section 1: Turning Market Days Into Vectors

To analyze structure mathematically, we need to represent market days as objects we can manipulate.

**One day = one vector.**

If you track 10 assets, then each day produces a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{10}$:

$$\mathbf{r}_t = \begin{pmatrix} r_{t,1} \\ r_{t,2} \\ \vdots \\ r_{t,10} \end{pmatrix}$$

where $r_{t,i}$ is the return of asset $i$ on day $t$.

**Many days = a matrix.**

Stack all your daily vectors as rows of a matrix:

$$R = \begin{pmatrix} — & \mathbf{r}_1^\top & — \\ — & \mathbf{r}_2^\top & — \\ & \vdots & \\ — & \mathbf{r}_T^\top & — \end{pmatrix}$$

This gives you a $T \times n$ matrix where $T$ is the number of days and $n$ is the number of assets.

## Section 2: Do Markets Explore All Directions?

We have 500 daily return vectors, each living in $\mathbb{R}^{10}$. In principle, these 500 vectors could point in **any** direction in 10-dimensional space.

But do they?

If market movements were truly random, the vectors would spread out to explore the full 10 dimensions. But if markets are driven by a few underlying factors (like interest rates, risk appetite, sector rotation), the vectors might cluster around a **smaller subspace**.

### 2.1 Measuring Effective Dimensionality

The **rank** of a matrix tells us how many linearly independent rows (or columns) it contains. For our returns matrix:

- If $\text{rank}(R) = 10$, the daily vectors span all 10 dimensions
- If $\text{rank}(R) = 3$, the daily vectors only span a 3-dimensional subspace

In practice, numerical rank is rarely exact due to noise. We'll look at something more informative: how the "importance" of each dimension falls off.

## Section 3: Identifying Dominant Movement Patterns

We've discovered that market movements live in a low-dimensional subspace. Now we want to **find** those dominant directions explicitly.

### 3.1 Why Standard Coordinates Hide Structure

When we look at returns, we see them in "asset coordinates": Asset 1, Asset 2, ..., Asset 10. But these coordinates are arbitrary—they're based on how we happened to organize our data, not on how the market actually moves.

Imagine the market primarily moves in two patterns:
- **Pattern A:** Tech and growth stocks move together
- **Pattern B:** Energy and value stocks move together

Neither pattern aligns with any single asset axis. In standard coordinates, these patterns are invisible. We need to **rotate** our view to see them.

### 3.2 Finding the Natural Basis

We want an **orthogonal basis** for the data—a new set of coordinate axes where:
1. The first axis points in the direction of greatest spread
2. The second axis points in the direction of second-greatest spread (perpendicular to the first)
3. And so on...

This is exactly what the SVD gives us. The right singular vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \ldots$ form this basis.

## Section 4: Visualizing Regimes in Reduced Space

Now comes the payoff. We've found that market movements live in a low-dimensional subspace and identified the dominant directions. Let's **project** our 500 daily vectors onto these directions and see what structure emerges.

### 4.1 Projection: From 10D to 2D

To project a return vector $\mathbf{r}$ onto the dominant directions, we compute:

$$\text{coordinate}_i = \mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}_i$$

This gives us the component of $\mathbf{r}$ along each dominant direction. If we keep only the first 2 or 3 coordinates, we get a 2D or 3D representation of each market day.

## Section 5: What Information Is Lost?

By projecting from 10 dimensions to 2 or 3, we're simplifying. Some information is preserved; some is lost. Understanding this tradeoff is crucial for any quant.

### 5.1 Reconstruction: Going Back

If we project a vector $\mathbf{r}$ onto $k$ directions and then try to reconstruct it:

$$\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}_i) \mathbf{v}_i$$

The reconstruction $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ contains only the information captured by those $k$ directions. The **residual** $\mathbf{r} - \hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is what we lost.

## Section 6: How Regimes Change Over Time

Markets don't stay in one regime forever. They transition—sometimes gradually, sometimes abruptly. Can we see these transitions geometrically?

### 6.1 Tracking Direction Over Time

In reduced space, each day is a point. If we connect consecutive days, we trace out a **trajectory** through regime space. Sharp turns in this trajectory indicate regime changes.
