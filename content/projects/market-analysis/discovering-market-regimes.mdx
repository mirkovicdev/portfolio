# Discovering Market Regimes Through Structure

Markets don't behave the same way every day. Sometimes everything moves together, up or down in unison. Other times, sectors rotate: tech falls while energy rises. These patterns repeat. Not exactly, but structurally.

Your task in this project is to **discover** whether market movements live in a few dominant patterns, and if so, to **see** those patterns as geometric structure. You will not predict anything. You will not optimize anything. You will simply look at real market behavior and ask: *what structure is hiding in these movements?*

The only tools you need are vectors, matrices, and the geometric intuition you've built.

---

## Section 0: What Is a Market Regime?

Before we touch any math, let's build intuition about what we're looking for.

Imagine watching the market over 500 trading days. Each day, you record how each of 10 assets moved, some up, some down, by various amounts. That's 500 observations of 10-dimensional behavior.

Now ask: **do those 500 days look random, or do they cluster into a few recurring patterns?**

A "regime" isn't about whether prices go up or down. It's about the **structure** of how assets move **together**. Two days belong to the same regime if their movements share the same underlying pattern, even if the magnitudes differ.

## Section 1: Turning Market Days Into Vectors

To analyze structure mathematically, we need to represent market days as objects we can manipulate.

**One day = one vector.**

If you track 10 assets, then each day produces a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{10}$:

$$\mathbf{r}_t = \begin{pmatrix} r_{t,1} \\ r_{t,2} \\ \vdots \\ r_{t,10} \end{pmatrix}$$

where $r_{t,i}$ is the return of asset $i$ on day $t$.

**Many days = a matrix.**

Stack all your daily vectors as rows of a matrix:

$$R = \begin{pmatrix} ,  & \mathbf{r}_1^\top & ,  \\ ,  & \mathbf{r}_2^\top & ,  \\ & \vdots & \\ ,  & \mathbf{r}_T^\top & ,  \end{pmatrix}$$

This gives you a $T \times n$ matrix where $T$ is the number of days and $n$ is the number of assets.

### Visualizing the Matrix Structure

If you mixed up rows and columns in the question above, you're not alone! It's one of the most common confusions in linear algebra. Let's make it crystal clear.

Here's a returns matrix $R$ with 3 days and 4 assets:

$$
R = \begin{pmatrix}
r_{1,1} & r_{1,2} & r_{1,3} & r_{1,4} \\
r_{2,1} & r_{2,2} & r_{2,3} & r_{2,4} \\
r_{3,1} & r_{3,2} & r_{3,3} & r_{3,4}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{array}{l}
\leftarrow \text{Day 1} \\
\leftarrow \text{Day 2} \\
\leftarrow \text{Day 3}
\end{array}
$$

$$
\underbrace{\text{Asset 1} \quad \text{Asset 2} \quad \text{Asset 3} \quad \text{Asset 4}}_{\text{columns}}
$$

**Reading the matrix:**
- **Row $i$** → all asset returns on day $i$ (one snapshot in time)
- **Column $j$** → asset $j$'s returns across all days (one asset's history)

So $r_{2,3}$ is the return of **Asset 3** on **Day 2**.

### What You Just Built

You've learned to **represent market data as geometric objects**. Here's what each piece does:

**Return vectors** (`np.array([r1, r2, ..., rn])`): A single day's returns become a point in n-dimensional space. If you have 10 assets, each day is a point in $\mathbb{R}^{10}$. This isn't just notation, it lets you use geometric intuition. Two days are "similar" if their points are close or point in the same direction.

**Returns matrix** (`np.vstack([day1, day2, ...])`): Stacking days as rows creates a (T × n) matrix where T is days and n is assets. Row i is day i. Column j is asset j's history. This organization is standard across finance and data science.

**Dimensions** (`R.shape`): Knowing (500, 10) means 500 observations of 10-dimensional behavior. This matters because 500 points in 10D space might reveal structure that's invisible when looking at individual assets.

**A concrete example**: Suppose on Day 1, tech stocks rose 2% and energy fell 1%. On Day 2, tech rose 4% and energy fell 2%. These vectors point in the *same direction*, tech up, energy down, just with different magnitudes. They represent the same "regime" (risk-on, growth-favoring). On Day 3, if tech fell 1% and energy rose 2%, that vector points in a *different direction*, a different regime.

**Why this matters**: This representation is universal. Every quant model, from simple correlation analysis to machine learning, starts with data in this matrix form. The geometric view (days as vectors, similarity as direction) is how practitioners actually think about market structure.

### 2.1 Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

The **Singular Value Decomposition** breaks any matrix into three parts:

$$R = U \Sigma V^T$$

The diagonal entries of $\Sigma$ are called **singular values**. They tell us the "importance" of each orthogonal direction, larger values mean more of the data's variance lies along that direction.

### What You Just Built

You've learned to **measure the intrinsic dimensionality of market data**. Here's what each piece does:

**Singular values** (`np.linalg.svd(R)[1]`): These numbers quantify how much the data spreads along each orthogonal direction. The first singular value measures spread along the most important direction, the second along the next most important (perpendicular to the first), and so on.

**Explained variance ratios** (`σᵢ² / Σσⱼ²`): Squaring and normalizing converts singular values into interpretable percentages. If the first ratio is 0.50, that direction alone explains 50% of all market movement. This is the same calculation used in PCA (Principal Component Analysis).

**A concrete example**: Suppose you analyze 10 sector ETFs and find singular values [8.2, 4.1, 2.0, 0.5, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1]. Squaring gives [67.2, 16.8, 4.0, ...]. The ratios are roughly [0.72, 0.18, 0.04, ...]. This means 72% of all sector movements can be explained by one direction (probably "overall market"), 18% by a second direction (perhaps "growth vs value"), and 4% by a third. The remaining 7 directions together explain only 6%, they're mostly noise.

**The key discovery**: Real markets typically have only 2-4 dominant directions, not 10. This is profound, it means thousands of stocks aren't moving independently. They're driven by a handful of underlying factors. This is why "regime" is a meaningful concept: there simply aren't that many distinct ways the market moves.

**Why this matters**: This analysis answers a fundamental question: "How complex is the market, really?" If 3 factors explain 90% of variance, you don't need to model 10 separate assets, you can model 3 factors and capture most of the behavior. This simplification is the foundation of factor investing, risk models, and portfolio optimization at every major fund.

## Section 3: Identifying Dominant Movement Patterns

We've discovered that market movements live in a low-dimensional subspace. Now we want to **find** those dominant directions explicitly.

### 3.1 Why Standard Coordinates Hide Structure

When we look at returns, we see them in "asset coordinates": Asset 1, Asset 2, ..., Asset 10. But these coordinates are arbitrary, they're based on how we happened to organize our data, not on how the market actually moves.

Imagine the market primarily moves in two patterns:
- **Pattern A:** Tech and growth stocks move together
- **Pattern B:** Energy and value stocks move together

Neither pattern aligns with any single asset axis. In standard coordinates, these patterns are invisible. We need to **rotate** our view to see them.

### 3.2 Finding the Natural Basis

We want an **orthogonal basis** for the data, a new set of coordinate axes where:
1. The first axis points in the direction of greatest spread
2. The second axis points in the direction of second-greatest spread (perpendicular to the first)
3. And so on...

This is exactly what the SVD gives us. The right singular vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \ldots$ form this basis. They're stored as **rows** of the matrix $V^T$.

### What You Just Built

You've learned to **extract and interpret the dominant movement patterns** in market data. Here's what each piece does:

**Extracting dominant directions** (`Vt[:k]`): The rows of Vt from SVD are the right singular vectors, orthonormal directions sorted by importance. The first row is the direction of maximum variance, the second is perpendicular to the first with maximum remaining variance, and so on. Slicing gives you the top k patterns.

**Projection onto a direction** (`np.dot(r, v)`): This measures how much a day's movement aligns with a pattern. If v represents "tech up, energy down" and today tech rose 3% while energy fell 2%, the projection is large and positive. If tech fell and energy rose, the projection is large and negative. If tech and energy both rose equally, the projection might be near zero (unrelated to this pattern).

**Variance along a direction** (`np.mean((R @ v) ** 2)`): This tells you how important a direction is across all days. High variance = the market frequently moves along this pattern. Low variance = this pattern rarely appears.

**A concrete example**: Suppose the first dominant direction is v₁ = [0.4, 0.4, 0.4, -0.3, -0.3, -0.3, 0, 0, 0, 0] (simplified). This says "tech stocks (first 3) move together, energy stocks (next 3) move opposite, others don't participate." Projecting a day onto v₁ gives a single number: positive means "risk-on" (tech up, energy down), negative means "risk-off" (tech down, energy up), near zero means "this day wasn't about that pattern."

**Interpreting directions**: Each dominant direction represents a "theme" or "factor" in market behavior:
- Direction 1 often captures "market" (everything moves together)
- Direction 2 might capture "growth vs value" or "risk-on vs risk-off"
- Direction 3 might capture sector rotation or interest rate sensitivity

**Why this matters**: This is exactly how factor models work in practice. Firms like MSCI Barra, Axioma, and Bloomberg build risk models by identifying these dominant directions and tracking how much each stock loads onto them. When you project returns onto dominant directions, you're decomposing "why did the market move today?" into interpretable components.

## Section 5: What Information Is Lost?

By projecting from 10 dimensions to 2 or 3, we're simplifying. Some information is preserved; some is lost. Understanding this tradeoff is crucial for any quant.

### 5.1 Reconstruction: Going Back

If we project a vector $\mathbf{r}$ onto $k$ directions and then try to reconstruct it:

$$\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}_i) \mathbf{v}_i$$

The reconstruction $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ contains only the information captured by those $k$ directions. The **residual** $\mathbf{r} - \hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is what we lost.

"Best" means it minimizes the sum of squared errors, no other k-dimensional subspace captures more variance. This is the Eckart-Young theorem, a fundamental result in linear algebra.

**Residuals** (`original - reconstructed`): These are the movements that don't fit the dominant patterns. If Apple had company-specific news that didn't affect other tech stocks, that movement lives in the residuals. Residuals aren't noise or errors, they're real movements that are just orthogonal to the common structure.

**Reconstruction quality**: If 3 components give you 90% quality, you've compressed 10 dimensions into 3 while keeping 90% of the information. The remaining 10% is idiosyncratic, asset-specific behavior.

**A concrete example**: Suppose your 10 assets are tech stocks. The first direction might be "tech sector moves together" (explains 60%). The second might be "large-cap vs small-cap tech" (explains 20%). The third might be "hardware vs software" (explains 10%). With k=3, you capture 90% of movements. The residual 10% is individual stock news, earnings surprises, CEO changes, product launches, that only affected one company.

**Why this matters**: This decomposition is the foundation of factor models used by every major quant fund. The dominant directions become your "factors" (like market, size, value in the Fama-French model). Residuals become "idiosyncratic risk" that diversification can eliminate. Understanding this split is essential for portfolio construction.

### What You Just Built

You now have tools to **detect when the market changes its behavior**. Here's what each piece does:

**Angle between vectors**: The formula $\cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2}{\|\mathbf{v}_1\| \|\mathbf{v}_2\|}$ measures directional similarity, ignoring magnitude. **Counting large transitions**: By setting a threshold (like 90°), you define what counts as a "regime change." If 5% of days exceed 90°, regimes are relatively stable. If 30% exceed 90°, the market is chaotic with frequent pattern shifts.

**Finding transition days**: Knowing *when* transitions occurred lets you correlate with real events. If you find a 150° angle on March 12, 2020, you can look up what happened, the WHO declared COVID a pandemic. The math identifies the anomaly; domain knowledge explains it.

**A concrete example**: In normal markets, consecutive days might have angles of 20°-40°, the market drifts gradually. But on August 5, 2024 (Yen carry trade unwind), the angle might spike to 140°, assets that usually moved together suddenly moved apart. This is a detectable regime break.

**Why this matters**: Regime detection is critical for risk management. Many trading strategies assume stable correlations, "if tech and growth usually move together, I'll hedge one with the other." When a regime breaks, those hedges fail catastrophically. Monitoring angle changes gives early warning. Funds like Bridgewater explicitly track regime changes to adjust their positions before losses compound.
