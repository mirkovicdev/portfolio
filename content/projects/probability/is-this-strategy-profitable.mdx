# Is This Trading Strategy Actually Profitable?

You have a simple trading strategy. It wins sometimes, loses sometimes, and pays costs every time. Your task is to decide whether it makes money in the long run.

This is not a math lesson. This is a quant decision. By the end of this project, you will either **trade it** or **not trade it**, and you will justify that decision with numbers.

Individual trades are noisy. A single winning trade proves nothing. A single losing trade proves nothing. What matters is **average outcome per trade, over many trades**. That's the only number that determines profitability.

---

## Section 0: The Task and the Rule for Decision-Making

Before writing any code, we need to be precise about what "profitable" means.

A strategy is profitable if, over many trades, you end up with more money than you started with. But luck can create short-term illusions, a string of wins in a losing strategy, or losses in a winning one. The only reliable measure cuts through the noise: **expected value per trade**.

## Section 1: Build the Edge Calculator (Expected Value)

We have probability and payoff vectors. Now we compute the one number that determines everything: **expected value per trade**.

Expected value is simply the dot product of probabilities and payoffs:

$$\text{EV} = p_{\text{win}} \times \text{payoff}_{\text{win}} + p_{\text{loss}} \times \text{payoff}_{\text{loss}}$$

### Generating Random Numbers in NumPy

To simulate trades, we need to generate random outcomes. NumPy provides `np.random.default_rng()` which creates a random number generator:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=42)  # seed for reproducibility
random_values = rng.random(10)        # 10 random floats between 0 and 1
```

To simulate a coin flip with probability `p` of heads:
- Generate a random number between 0 and 1
- If it's less than `p`, call it heads (1); otherwise tails (0)

```python
p = 0.6  # 60% chance of heads
```

## Section 4: Stress Testing

Your strategy has positive expected value. Congratulations, on paper. But paper ignores reality.

**Stress testing** asks: does your edge survive when things go wrong? Real markets have frictions, surprises, and costs that your simple model doesn't capture. A strategy that looks profitable in theory can be destroyed by real-world conditions.

### Why Stress Test?

The expected value we computed assumes:
- You always execute at the exact price you want
- Losses never exceed your modeled amount
- Your win probability is exactly what you estimated

None of these are true. Stress testing adjusts your EV calculation to account for realistic degradation.

### Friction 1: Slippage

**Slippage** is the difference between the price you expect and the price you actually get. You want to buy at \$100, but by the time your order executes, the price is \$100.15. That's \$0.15 of slippage.

Slippage happens because:
- Markets move while your order is being processed
- Large orders push prices against you
- Low liquidity means worse execution

**How to model it:** Slippage is an additional cost on every trade. If average slippage is \$2 per trade, subtract \$2 from your expected value.

$$\text{EV}_{\text{adjusted}} = \text{EV}_{\text{base}} - \text{avg\_slippage}$$

### Friction 2: Tail Risk

**Tail risk** refers to rare but catastrophic events that your basic model ignores. Your strategy assumes losses are capped at \$80, but what if a flash crash causes a \$500 loss? What if it happens 2% of the time?

These "black swan" events can wipe out months of gains in a single trade.

**How to model it:** Add a third outcome to your probability model:
- With probability $p_{\text{win}} \times (1 - p_{\text{tail}})$: normal win
- With probability $(1 - p_{\text{win}}) \times (1 - p_{\text{tail}})$: normal loss
- With probability $p_{\text{tail}}$: catastrophic tail loss

### Exercise 2: Add Tail Risk

Tail risk is trickier to model than slippage. We need to modify our probability distribution to include a third outcome: the catastrophic loss.

The key insight: when a tail event occurs, it *replaces* what would have been a normal win or loss. So we scale down the original probabilities by $(1 - p_{\text{tail}})$ to make room for the tail event:
